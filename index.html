Engineering reference · Living roadmap

System design for the engineer who now also owns the model.

Two skill sets have quietly merged. The person who used to be asked “how would you shard this table” is now also asked “why is your p95 time-to-first-token 4 seconds and your GPU bill forty thousand dollars a month.” This roadmap covers both halves as one subject, because in production they are one subject.

Every topic answers the same four questions: what it is, why it exists (the problem that forced someone to invent it), how it fails, and what a senior engineer is expected to say about it out loud. Diagrams are schematic — they show the control flow that matters, not a deployment topology.

How to read this

  • Tick a topic when you could explain it to someone else, including its failure modes — not when you have read it. Progress is stored in this browser.
  • Parts 01–10 are the classical distributed-systems core. Parts 11–15 are the AI infrastructure layer. Part 16 is where you rehearse both together, and Part 17 is what to read next.
  • One theme recurs throughout: bound your resources, make operations idempotent, and make failure explicit rather than silent.
  • Every time estimate is computed from the content, not guessed. Read is a careful first pass at 170 words a minute, plus time to study each diagram and to attempt each question before reading its answer. Deep is the optional extra: following the references, running the checklist against a system you own, and typing out the code.

A closing note. Every component in this roadmap is also a new failure domain. Add each one because a measured problem demands it, not because the architecture diagram looks incomplete without it. The same rule now applies to models, vector indexes and agents.
